Automated Box Kitting for High Mix, High Volume Warehouse Operations

Background

Warehousing operations remain heavily dependent on manual kitting processes, where operators are required to perform repetitive tasks such as box preparation, SKU handling, and product insertion. These manual processes are labour-intensive, difficult to scale, and prone to human error, resulting in inconsistent throughput and reduced operational efficiency.

In high-mix environments, where product dimensions and kitting configurations frequently change, conventional automation systems are often unsuitable due to their reliance on fixed tooling and predefined motion sequences. This lack of flexibility limits their ability to adapt to varying product requirements without significant reconfiguration or downtime. The objective was to develop a flexible automation solution capable of supporting multiple product types and kitting configurations while maintaining high throughput, positional accuracy, and reliable system operation.

In this project, I was responsible for the design and implementation of the control system, robot programming, and overall system integration for an automated multi-product box kitting system within a constrained workspace. This includes developing PLC control logic using CODESYS, programming the KUKA SCARA manipulator for pick-and-place operations, and integrating industrial communication protocols such as Profinet, Modbus TCP, and IO-Link to coordinate the interaction between mechanical subsystems, sensors, actuators, and the manipulator.

Project Objectives

  • Develop a PLC-based control system using CODESYS to coordinate the kitting process.
  • Program the KUKA SCARA manipulator for high-speed pick-and-place operations (< 4s cycle time).
  • Enable flexible operation across 8 product types and 3 kitting configurations.
  • Achieve ≥ 900 boxes/hour throughput with < 0.04% error rate.

System Implementation

The automated box kitting system is implemented using a PLC-based control architecture developed in the CODESYS platform, integrating multiple subsystems including the Box Alignment System, SKU Alignment System, and the KUKA SCARA robot. Communication between controllers and devices is achieved using industrial protocols such as Profinet, Modbus TCP, IO-Link, and digital I/O to ensure reliable real-time operation.

The system follows a hierarchical control structure. The HMI with an integrated PLC functions as the master controller, coordinating the overall kitting sequence, while dedicated PLCs are assigned to the Box Alignment and SKU Alignment subsystems to handle low-level control of sensors and pneumatic actuators. This distributed approach reduces computational load on the master controller and ensures deterministic execution of real-time tasks.

PLC programs are developed using Structured Text (ST) in CODESYS. Each subsystem PLC executes sequential logic to control actuator timing based on sensor feedback. Photoelectric sensors are interfaced using digital inputs, while pneumatic actuators are controlled through digital outputs connected to solenoid valves. The logic ensures that only one box and the correct number of SKUs are positioned at their respective stations before allowing the kitting process to proceed.

Communication between the master controller and subsystem PLCs is implemented using Profinet, allowing fast and deterministic exchange of status signals such as box readiness and SKU readiness. These signals are used to synchronise subsystem operations and trigger the pick-and-place sequence only when all conditions are met.

The KUKA SCARA robot is integrated using Modbus TCP communication. The master controller transmits key parameters such as kitting configuration, SKU dimensions, box dimensions, and readiness signals to the robot. The robot operates in a continuous loop, waiting for these signals before executing pick-and-place actions.

The electric parallel gripper is controlled using an IO-Link interface, enabling bidirectional communication between the PLC and the gripper. Control commands such as open and close positions are transmitted as WORD values, while feedback signals are received to verify gripping status. A calibration model is implemented in the PLC to convert SKU dimensions into gripper control values.

A waypoint compensation algorithm is implemented within the control logic to dynamically adjust robot positions based on SKU and box dimensions. Instead of using fixed waypoints for each product type, the final robot position is computed by combining a base programmed waypoint with calculated offsets and user-defined corrections. This allows the system to handle multiple product configurations without requiring separate robot programs.

During operation, the subsystem PLCs executes a sequential workflow. Once the box and SKU are detected at their respective stations, a ready signal is sent to the robot. The robot moves to the pick position, activates the gripper to grasp the SKU, lifts the product, and moves to the kitting station. The product is then inserted into the box, and the gripper is released. Upon completion, the robot sends a completion signal back to the master controller, allowing the next cycle to begin.

Safety is handled using a dedicated Safety PLC, which operates independently of the control logic. The emergency stop is hardwired to the robot through relay outputs, ensuring immediate shutdown of robot motion regardless of software state. The safety status is also transmitted to the master controller to halt system operation when triggered.

Challenges and Solutions

Mechanical Tolerances

One of the key challenges encountered during system implementation was the accumulation of mechanical tolerances arising from manual adjustments of guide rails and alignment mechanisms in both the Box Alignment System and SKU Alignment System. These adjustments are necessary to accommodate different box and SKU dimensions, but introduce small positional deviations from the ideal reference points required for accurate kitting.

Although the system is designed to constrain boxes and SKUs to fixed reference positions using pneumatic actuators and mechanical stoppers, variations in setup such as slight misalignment of guide rails, uneven contact surfaces, and tolerance stack-up across mechanical components result in positional offsets at the pick and placement locations. These deviations, while small, lead to collisions during the insertion process.

To address this issue, a user-defined waypoint offset mechanism was implemented within the robot control logic. This allows fine-tuning of the robot’s pick and place positions along the X, Y, and Z axes. The offsets can be adjusted in increments of 0.01 mm through the HMI interface, enabling precise compensation for mechanical deviations without modifying the KUKA robot program.

The final robot position is computed as the sum of the programmed waypoint, calculated compensation based on product dimensions, and the user-defined offset. This approach maintains system flexibility while ensuring high positional accuracy, allowing the system to consistently perform kitting operations despite variations introduced during mechanical setup.

Conclusion

The automated box kitting system was successfully developed and integrated to meet the requirements of a high-mix warehouse environment. The system achieved a cycle time of 3 seconds per pick-and-place operation and a throughput of up to 1200 boxes per hour for single and duo kitting configurations, while maintaining consistent and reliable operation during testing.

Throughout this project, I was responsible for the development of the PLC control logic using CODESYS, system integration between multiple subsystems, and the implementation of industrial communication protocols including Profinet, Modbus TCP, and IO-Link. I also developed key algorithms such as gripper parameter calculation and waypoint compensation, enabling the system to operate flexibly across different kitting configurations.

This project demonstrates my ability to design and implement industrial automation systems, perform system-level integration, and develop practical engineering solutions to address real-world constraints such as mechanical tolerances, timing synchronisation, and system scalability.
